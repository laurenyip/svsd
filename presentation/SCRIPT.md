# SOUNDBOOK — presentation screenplay (draft 1)

**Runtime target:** 6:00 (hard cap 7:00) · **Narration:** ~830 words at a relaxed ~140 wpm
**What they grade:** (1) picking the right problems, (2) depth of thinking on them, (3) craft of the result.
Every scene below is there to earn one of those three. The tag on each scene says which.

**Formats used**
- `SCREEN`: screen recording of the prototype (record at 1440×900, cursor hidden unless noted)
- `CAM`: me on camera, talking head
- `SLIDE`: one simple full-bleed card in the Soundbook style (paper or yellow, Bricolage headline)
- `VO`: voice-over over whatever is on screen
- `[ ]`: things to fill in or check before filming

Burn captions into the final cut. A video about deaf kids should be watchable without sound.

## SCENE 1: COLD OPEN (0:00–0:25) · *craft*

`SCREEN`: Story page, snake. Ghost snake breathing on the dotted paper. A child-height voice (mine, softly) says
"The snake says sssss…" Soft rings of sticker dots turn slowly in the middle of the page. Ripples run through them with the voice, dots light up in mint and sky and glide out, then fly into the snake. It peels off the
page as a sticker. The flower blinks, then squints happily: *you did it!*

> **VO:** This snake was drawn by a voice. Not by tapping or tracing, but by the sound "s".
> I want to show you how I got here, and mostly the problems I had to solve along the way.

`SLIDE`: **Soundbook**: *a picture book you draw with your voice.*

## SCENE 2: WHO IT'S FOR (0:25–1:05) · *right problem*

`CAM`

> **ON CAMERA:** The prompt pointed me toward deaf kids. But "deaf kids" is too broad for one prototype.
> Some families sign, some use spoken language, and some do both. A five-year-old and a fifteen-year-old
> need completely different things.
>
> So I narrowed it down: children around four to eight, with hearing aids or cochlear implants, in speech
> therapy, whose families have chosen spoken language as a goal. And there's always a grown-up beside them,
> a parent or a speech-language pathologist.

`SLIDE`: three stacked stickers with labels: **4–8 years** · **hearing aids / CI** · **a grown-up beside them**

> **VO:** That last part matters. It changes the product from a game a child plays alone into a tool
> two people use together.

## SCENE 3: THE REAL PROBLEM (1:05–1:45) · *right problem / depth*

`SLIDE`: a simple frequency bar from low to high. "sh" sits in the middle, "s" sits far right, and a soft shaded
band over the high end is labelled *often hardest to hear*.

> **VO:** Here's the insight the whole thing rests on. "S" is one of the hardest sounds for these kids,
> and not because it's hard to make. It's hard to *hear*. Its energy sits very high, roughly four to eight
> kilohertz. That's often where hearing loss is greatest and where hearing technology gives the least.
> `[verify the wording with an SLP or audiology source before filming]`
>
> Normally you learn a sound by hearing yourself make it. If you can't hear your own "s" clearly,
> that loop is broken. So the question became:
> **can the screen close that loop and let a child *see* their own sound?**

## SCENE 4: INSPIRATION (1:45–2:10) · *depth*

`SCREEN`: split. Left: `[the X clip: a performer's movement turned into particles]`. Right: Apple Watch Breathe.
Small inset: motionary's *particle onboarding* (dust morphing into shapes) and *voice orb* (a dot cloud that ripples with the mic).

> **VO:** Two references shaped the feel. One was this clip, where a performer's movement becomes a
> cloud of particles. It's expressive, and you *are* the drawing. The other was Apple Watch Breathe.
> It guides you without ever scoring you. You just follow the flower.
> I wanted both: a child's voice becomes material, and something calm guides it, without a score.
>
> My first version sprayed particles up from the text, like a fountain. It felt like the voice was *spending*
> something. So I switched to rings of dots that always live on the page, turning and breathing. The voice doesn't
> create the dots, it *wakes* them. The page is never empty, and silence is never a dead screen.

---

## SCENE 5: FOUR PROBLEMS I HAD TO SOLVE (2:10–4:30) · *depth (the core of the talk)*

### 5a. What do you even visualise? (2:10–2:45)

`SCREEN`: hold "s" with the pointer on the right side, then slide left to "sh". The lit dots change from small, quick,
mint, sky and sunshine dots to larger, slower violet, pink and lilac ones. Speak louder: more dots wake, and ripples run through the rings.

> **VO:** My first instinct was pitch or loudness, but neither one separates "s" from "sh". What does is *where
> the hiss energy sits*: higher for "s", lower for "sh". So that one number drives the particles' character.
> "S" becomes fine bright sparks and "sh" becomes soft broad drifts. Loudness only decides *how many* dots wake up.
> That way a child can see the difference between their own two sounds, before any target is involved.

### 5b. Who decides what "right" is? (2:45–3:15)

`SCREEN`: grown-up taps "say 'sss' once". The ghost snake fades in, tinted the colour of that sound.

> **VO:** There's no universal correct "s". Every child, mic and room is different. So the app never judges
> against a lab recording. The grown-up says the sound once, and *that* becomes the target, shown as
> a faint ghost of the picture. It's personal to this child, in this room, today. And it keeps the adult in the loop,
> because therapy is a relationship, not a quiz.

### 5c. Feedback without failure (3:15–3:55)

`SCREEN`: slow pointer drag from far left toward the right. Particles scatter, then curve toward the snake, then settle.
The status chip moves through "Keep going…", "Getting warmer…" and "So close — hold it!". The flower opens.

> **VO:** This is the moment I care most about. There's no red X and no "wrong". As the sound moves toward the
> target, the particles drift toward the picture. As it moves away, they scatter. Only closer or further.
>
> That's kinder, but it's also more *honest*. Sound detection is never perfect, so a yes-or-no verdict would
> sometimes tell a child they failed when they didn't. A gradient matches what the software actually knows.

### 5d. Making the picture tell the truth (3:55–4:30)

`SCREEN`: debug moment. Make "shhh" (particles fly), then switch quickly to "sss".
`[optional: show the before/after of the bug as two short clips]`

> **VO:** One bug taught me a design rule. Dots lit by a *wrong* sound were still in the air when the child
> switched to the right one, and they settled into the snake. The drawing showed something the child
> didn't do. So now a dot only becomes part of the picture if it was lit by a close sound, and a page needs
> a held sound, not one lucky moment. The illustration has to be a true record of the voice.

## SCENE 6: THE BOOK IS THE RECORD (4:30–5:05) · *right problem / craft*

`SCREEN`: page finishes, picture lifts off as a sticker, page turns to the ship, then "The end!" with both stickers,
then *My book* with the strip of stickers over dates.

> **VO:** Speech therapy is slow. Progress happens over weeks, and kids don't read charts. So every finished
> page is kept as a sticker, drawn in the child's own colours, with the date and how many seconds of sound
> it took. For the child it's a sticker book. For the parent and therapist it's a progress record.
> When that number gets smaller over the weeks, that's the story.

---

## SCENE 7: CRAFT CHOICES (5:05–5:35) · *craft*

`SCREEN`: quick cuts: the cover with its bobbing stickers, the multicolour "sss", the flower blinking, the line of text.

> **VO:** On the visual side, I moved from a dark, glowing night sky to paper and stickers. It feels warmer,
> more like a real book, and stickers are a reward kids already understand. The line the child reads is set in
> Lexend, a typeface designed for reading ease. The playful parts use Bricolage Grotesque. The grown-up layer,
> like dates and page numbers, is in a quieter serif, so the two audiences don't compete. And nothing depends
> on hearing a sound cue: every bit of feedback is visual.

---

## SCENE 8: SCOPE & NEXT (5:35–6:00) · *depth*

`CAM`

> **ON CAMERA:** I deliberately scoped this to "s" versus "sh", because their difference is clear enough to
> detect reliably. Sounds like "f" and "th" are much harder to tell apart, even for software, and I'd rather
> name them as future work than fake them.
>
> Next I'd put it in front of a speech therapist and a few families, tune the thresholds with real
> hearing-aid and implant users, and test it in noisy living rooms. Thanks for watching.

`SLIDE`: end card, the snake and ship stickers on yellow. **Soundbook.**
