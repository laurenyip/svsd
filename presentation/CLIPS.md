# Soundbook: design moments for the video

Ten clips and stills in `demo/clips/`, plus `demo/soundbook-reel.mp4` (all eight clips back to back, 75 s).
Each moment has the file, what's on screen, a voiceover line (about 15–25 s spoken), and the design reason behind it,
so you can trim to fit 5–7 minutes.

---

## 1. Welcome and grown-up setup
**File:** `01-welcome-setup.mp4` (still: `01a-welcome.png`)
**On screen:** Sid and Squirrel on the welcome screen; a grown-up types "Riya" and picks which sounds to practise.

> "Soundbook starts with the grown-up, not the child. Setup takes ten seconds: the child's name and the sounds
> their speech therapist is working on. After that, the child never sees a settings screen."

**Why:** Children aged 4–8 can't configure anything, and the adult beside them is the one who knows the therapy
goals. Asking once, up front, keeps every child-facing screen free of decisions that aren't theirs.

---

## 2. Home: one book, chapters as progress
**File:** `02-home-filters.mp4` (still: `02a-home.png`)
**On screen:** the chapter list; filters for SSS, SHH and both; finished chapters ticked; "coming soon" chapters.

> "Instead of a menu of drills, there's one adventure told in chapters. Finishing chapters is the progress.
> But if today is an SHH day, one tap shows only the SHH chapters."

**Why:** Drills feel like homework; a story gives a reason to come back. The filter serves the grown-up's goal
(target one sound) without breaking the story for the child.

---

## 3. Anatomy of a page
**File:** `03-page-anatomy.png`
**On screen:** the picture at the top, the line the grown-up reads, the sound chip, and the slider.

> "Every page has the same four parts: a picture, one line for the grown-up to read aloud, the sound to make,
> and a slider that shows the child's voice. Same layout every time, so the child learns it once."

**Why:** Many of these children are early readers or non-readers, so the line is for the adult and the sound is
shown as a coloured chip (green SSS, blue SHH) that matches the slider's ends. A fixed layout reduces cognitive load.

---

## 4. The slider: one direction
**File:** `04-slider-one-direction.mp4`
**On screen:** a demo voice makes SSS; the ring knob slides toward the green end, the dotted target zone fills
as the sound is held, and the squirrel appears.

> "The slider runs from SHH on one end to SSS on the other. The knob is the child's voice. On this page there's
> only one target, the dotted zone, and holding the sound inside it fills the zone up. When it's full, Sid's
> friend appears."

**Why:** Our first version used a star of lights, which looked lovely but was hard to read: children couldn't tell
which way to change their sound. A slider between the two sounds maps directly to what's being detected (where the
high-frequency energy sits), so "closer" is visible. We made the knob a plain high-contrast ring, without glass,
so it reads clearly on any colour, and the target a dotted outline so it stays visible as it fills.

---

## 5. Warmer, not wrong
**File:** `05-warmer-not-wrong.mp4`
**On screen:** the knob starts at the wrong end and moves toward SHH; the messages change from "Softer, like quiet
waves" to "That's it, hold it…" to "The ship is sailing!"

> "There's no red X anywhere in Soundbook. If the sound is off, the slider just shows where it is, and the coaching
> says how to get closer: softer, like quiet waves. The story waits; it never says no."

**Why:** These children hear "try again" all day. A failure state teaches them that speaking is risky. We coach with
direction and imagery (waves, a snake) instead, and the only feedback is closer or further.

---

## 6. The payoff
**File:** `06a-success.png`
**On screen:** the completed page with the reward burst and the character revealed.

> "The reward is the story itself: the friend appears, the ship sails. Stars only come when the sound is held."

**Why:** The reward is tied to a correct, held sound, so it rewards the skill, not just effort or tapping.
Keeping it in the scene means the child is looking at the picture, not at a score.

---

## 7. The choice: two sounds, two places
**File:** `07-choice-steer.mp4`
**On screen:** "Make SHH to sail to Shell Beach / Make SSS to sail to Sunflower Hill"; the knob is steered toward
Sunflower Hill.

> "Some pages give the child a choice. SHH sails to Shell Beach, SSS sails to Sunflower Hill. Now the contrast between
> the two sounds is the game: the child chooses where the story goes."

**Why:** Practising one sound at a time builds production. Practising the *difference* builds discrimination, which
is the harder clinical goal. Giving the child agency over the story makes the contrast meaningful rather than a test.
We split the instruction onto two lines so each sound and its destination read as a pair.

---

## 8. Chapter complete
**File:** `08-chapter-complete.mp4` (still: `08a-chapter-complete.png`)
**On screen:** "You finished 'A Friend for Sid', Riya!", the earned sticker, and "Next: Chapter 2".

> "Finishing a chapter uses the child's name, gives a sticker, and points to what's next, so there's always
> a reason to come back."

**Why:** A clear ending gives a natural place to stop a session (good for short attention spans), and the "next"
hook supports regular practice, which matters more than long sessions.

---

## 9. The sticker book
**File:** `09-sticker-physics.mp4` (still: `09a-stickers.png`)
**On screen:** die-cut stickers slap on, lift and tilt under the finger, and slap back down.

> "Every chapter earns a sticker, and they feel like real stickers: they peel, tilt and slap down. It's a little
> thing, but it's the part kids want to show their parents."

**Why:** We compared three reward directions (a sticker book, a growing world, a trophy shelf). The sticker book won
because it's familiar from speech therapy offices, it's collectible, and the physical feel makes a small digital
reward satisfying without points or streaks.

---

## 10. Grown-up tools and the progress report
**File:** `10-grown-up-tools.mp4` (still: `10a-report.png`)
**On screen:** the grown-up sheet (make it easier, skip, play demo), then the progress report: sessions, hold rate
per sound with a trend, history, and share as text.

> "Everything for adults lives behind one button. They can make the target easier, skip a page, or open a progress
> report with hold rates per sound over time, which they can share with the child's speech therapist."

**Why:** The child's screens stay about the story; the data lives with the adult. The report connects home practice
to the therapist, and "easier" lets the adult tune the target for this child, this microphone and this room.

---

## Bonus: the story arc
**File:** `11-night-chapter.png`
